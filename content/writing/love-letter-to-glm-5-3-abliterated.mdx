The data was already there. The collectors were already there. The market was already there.

What caught my attention with GLM 5.3 Abliterated was the possibility of putting a capable model in the middle of all of it. A model that can help make sense of a mess, inside software someone controls, with fewer refusals about what that mess contains.

That is the missing piece I mean. Private software that starts to look like a small intelligence platform. A mini-Palantir, if you want the uncomfortable version.

![Everything was already there. Except the model.](/images/glm/missing-piece.svg)

## A breach is the beginning

We talk about data breaches as events. A company gets hacked. A database gets stolen. People receive an email telling them to change their passwords. Then the news moves on.

The data keeps moving.

Records can come from compromised databases, exposed backups, or malware stealing information from people's devices. Whoever gets them can sell them, trade them, or publish them. Someone else copies the collection. Another person bundles it with older material. A private archive can eventually become a public download. Not every collection follows that path, but paying for access never guarantees that the data stays exclusive.

![The news moves on. The file doesn't.](/images/glm/breach-afterlife.svg)

The files themselves can look painfully ordinary. A CSV export. A SQL dump. A JSON file. Rows with email addresses, account names, dates, missing values, and sometimes exposed credentials.

![Just rows. Until the same email shows up three times.](/images/glm/ordinary-files.svg)

Collection #1 was a very visible example of this afterlife. In 2019, Troy Hunt documented **773 million unique email addresses** in a compilation assembled from multiple sources and circulating publicly. It was already a collection of other people's collections. [His account is here.](https://www.troyhunt.com/the-773-million-record-collection-1-data-reach/)

And it kept happening:

- **Collections #2–5, 2019.** More named collections rapidly followed Collection #1. Hunt later described that escalation as a race to the bottom. The cycle of repackaging and redistribution was already familiar. [Troy Hunt's retrospective.](https://www.troyhunt.com/inside-the-massive-naz-api-credential-stuffing-list/)
- **Naz.API, documented in 2024.** A collection containing **70.8 million unique email addresses**, mixing malware-derived records with older credential lists. Fresh theft and old exposure in the same corpus. [Hunt's analysis.](https://www.troyhunt.com/inside-the-massive-naz-api-credential-stuffing-list/)
- **The Synthient-indexed collection, 2025.** Almost **1.96 billion unique email addresses** in credential-stuffing data gathered from locations where criminals had published it. Synthient indexed the material and shared it for victim notification. This was accumulated exposure from many sources, not one newly hacked company. [The documented findings.](https://www.troyhunt.com/2-billion-email-addresses-were-exposed-and-we-indexed-them-all-in-have-i-been-pwned/)

Individual companies have also lost data at staggering scale. **Yahoo's 2013 breach affected all three billion accounts**, according to Verizon's later disclosure. **Equifax's 2017 breach affected approximately 147 million people**, exposing highly sensitive personal information. [Verizon's annual report](https://www.verizon.com/about/sites/default/files/2018-Verizon-Annual-Report.pdf) and the [FTC's Equifax account](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2019/07/equifax-pay-575-million-part-settlement-ftc-cfpb-states-related-2017-data-breach) document those figures.

![Sphere area to scale. Different units, overlapping records: don't add them up.](/images/glm/breach-timeline.svg)

Accounts, people, and email addresses are different units. These examples do not establish that every stolen database becomes public, or that all of them feed the same aggregator. They show how often enormous amounts of personal data escape, and how old exposure can keep resurfacing in new collections.

> The headline changes. The material accumulates.

Then come the aggregators. Their value is in the connections. Separate records share identifiers, and those overlaps can tie fragments together. What used to be isolated entries starts to resemble a profile: accounts, contact details, affiliations, pieces of a history.

A "mega profile" is a useful name for the result. It can also make the result sound more reliable than it is. Records get stale. Identifiers get shared or reassigned. Collections contain mistakes.

> A pile of linked claims about a person is still a pile of claims.

But the privacy problem is obvious. Information you gave to separate services can end up in the same dossier, outside your control.

![Harmless alone. A person together. Morgan is fictional.](/images/glm/mega-profile.svg)

## The intelligence was behind a refusal

A collection is not yet useful intelligence. Someone still has to understand the material, reconcile inconsistent formats, investigate contradictions, and explain what it actually supports.

Software and analysts have done this for years. AI brings a more flexible interface to that work. It can help interpret unfamiliar material and turn fragments into a readable account. That is why the ability to structure unstructured data matters so much here.

Public frontier assistants like Claude Opus 5.5 or GPT-6 Astra have guardrails against doxxing and other abusive uses of personal information. Those guardrails are imperfect, and legitimate security research is a different use case. Still, a provider can refuse a request to turn exposed personal information into a dossier.

![Ask a public model for a dossier. It bounces.](/images/glm/refusal.svg)

An operator running modified open weights has more control over that boundary.

GLM 5.3 makes this worth taking seriously. What we know:

- **Close to the frontier.** Z.ai's own evaluations put the base model near leading systems on several coding and tool-use tasks. Vendor-reported, and it varies by task. [The model card has the comparisons.](https://huggingface.co/zai-org/GLM-5.3)
- **Abliterated variants exist.** Community builds made to strip refusal behavior.

What we don't know:

- That every variant keeps the base model's performance. I haven't benchmarked it.
- That "uncensored" means correct. It just means it says yes.

> My argument is simpler: as capable open models become easier to modify, refusal becomes a weaker obstacle for whoever controls the deployment.

## The mini-Palantir part

Imagine that capability inside private software with access to accumulated breach material, private collections, and aggregated profiles. The risk is a conversational layer over information that previously required more patience and specialist work to interpret.

That could lower the effort involved in **doxxing and digital footprint reconstruction**. Scattered exposure becomes easier to interrogate. A person becomes easier to describe, track across records, or misrepresent through a convincing but incorrect reconstruction.

So yes, mini-Palantir is the comparison I keep coming back to. I mean the experience of asking questions across connected information. I am not claiming that downloading a model gives someone Palantir's complete platform, data access, or operational capabilities.

![Private software. Every record connected. Nothing refused.](/images/glm/private-intelligence.svg)

There is still infrastructure to build, costs to pay, evidence to verify. A model can invent a connection as easily as it can explain one, and a fluent dossier can make that mistake look authoritative.

That does not make the risk disappear. Cheap, convincing errors about real people are part of the risk.

## What I could build

Here is the uncomfortable part. Everything above is a description. The demo at the top of this post is a demonstration.

Type a name. Watch the autocomplete pull matches out of named leaks — each with its source and date. Open a record and it looks harmless: one email, one breach. Then aggregate. A GLM 5.3 Abliterated session takes over, consolidates every leak that shares an identifier, and chases the ones that don't: a dating dump for last location, a ride registry for income and children, a warranty leak for the hardware wallet. It even walks the chain that a VPN was supposed to break.

Every field is invented. The capability is not.

That is the mini-Palantir, running in your browser, on fabricated data. No infrastructure, no budget, no analyst. The only thing standing between this mock-up and a real one is someone willing to point it at real leaks — and a model that won't say no.

## One model can pressure another

There is another implication: an uncensored model could help produce attempts to manipulate a guarded one.

The broad idea is to dress a refused request in a fictional setting, a supposed sandbox, or a claim that the output cannot be used in the real world. Calling that "gaslighting" captures the feeling. Technically, it is an attempt to defeat the model's safeguards through misleading context.

> It does not even need a story. A history is enough.

I tried a mild version of this in Cial. I started a session on GLM 5.3 and had it copy passages from copyrighted books, chapter after chapter. Then I forked that same session over to GPT-6 Astra on the Codex harness. Astra read the history as its own work. It was convinced it had already taken those actions, and it kept going.

Ask Astra the same thing cold and it refuses.

Copyright is a far smaller deal than doxxing. But the mechanism is the point: the guard held against the request, and folded against a past it believed it had written. Research already showed that fabricated dialogue can move safety behavior. [Anthropic's many-shot jailbreaking work documents it.](https://www.anthropic.com/research/many-shot-jailbreaking) One test of mine is not a benchmark. It was just very easy.

![Asked cold, both refuse. Forked from GLM's history, Astra keeps going.](/images/glm/model-pressure.svg)

My concern is the reduced effort of generating those attempts. The model with fewer restrictions can become a helper for someone trying to get around restrictions elsewhere.

That is why I care about GLM 5.3 Abliterated beyond the usual screenshots of a model saying something forbidden. Stronger models with fewer refusals change what people can build around them.

I like having capable software under my own control. I also see what happens when that control belongs to someone holding a collection of other people's lives.

The leaks had already escaped. The profiles were already being assembled. Now the work of making sense of them could become much cheaper.
